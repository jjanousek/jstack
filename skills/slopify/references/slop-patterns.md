# Slop pattern catalogue

Use this catalogue to find clusters of problems. Do not treat it as a detector or a list of forbidden words.

## 1. Diagnostic rule

Suspect slop when several familiar patterns appear together and the text still contains little that is specific, useful, or verifiable.

Ask:

- What does this passage tell the reader that they did not already know?
- Which claim could the reader check?
- Could the same paragraph fit a different company, product, or topic with only two nouns changed?
- If the headings, bullets, bolding, and transitions disappeared, what information would remain?
- Is the text making a decision, giving an instruction, reporting evidence, or merely sounding complete?

Authorship is a separate question. Style alone cannot answer it reliably.

## 2. Substance problems

### Empty competence

The prose sounds informed but names no action, mechanism, constraint, result, or decision.

Weak: “A comprehensive strategy enables meaningful progress across the organisation.”

Better: “The new queue assigns each request to an owner and shows its status.”

### Unsupported importance

The text announces that something is important, pivotal, transformative, or unprecedented instead of showing the consequence.

Repair by stating what changed and who is affected.

### Vague attribution

Phrases such as “research shows,” “experts agree,” or “industry leaders increasingly” borrow authority without supplying a source.

Repair by naming and linking the source, identifying the claim as an observation, or removing it.

### Missing specifics

Results are “significant,” timing is “recent,” and improvements happen “over time.” The reader cannot learn from or verify the claim.

Do not invent specifics. Request them, flag the gap, or use a visible placeholder such as `[add measured result]`.

### Plausible but fabricated specificity

A precise-looking number or anecdote is worse than a vague sentence when it is invented. Specificity must come from supplied evidence or be clearly hypothetical.

### Automatic significance explanation

The text repeatedly says “this matters because” or “the key takeaway is” when the facts should carry the point.

Keep the explanation only when the consequence is not obvious to the intended reader.

## 3. Word and phrase habits

### Inflated verbs and nouns

Plain actions are replaced by ceremonial language: use becomes leverage, help becomes empower, fix becomes remediate, plan becomes initiative, and change becomes transformation.

Keep technical terms when they are precise. Replace them only when a simpler word means the same thing.

### Decorative modifiers

Words such as truly, deeply, incredibly, robust, seamless, dynamic, meaningful, and cutting-edge often add mood without information.

Delete one and check whether the claim changes.

### Defensive hedging

Multiple qualifiers protect a weak claim: arguably, perhaps, generally, relatively, it may be worth noting.

Keep uncertainty that is real. Remove stacked caution that prevents the sentence from taking a position.

### Throat-clearing

The writer announces the next point instead of making it: “It is important to note,” “the key point is,” “when it comes to,” or “I would be happy to explain.”

Start with the point.

### Stock scene-setters

Openings about a fast-changing world, an evolving landscape, or unprecedented times can introduce almost any subject.

Start with the event, problem, or question specific to this piece.

### Manufactured pivots

“Here is where it gets interesting,” “but here is the thing,” and similar turns manufacture drama before an ordinary observation.

Delete the pivot and state the observation.

### False contrasts

“It is not X; it is Y” can make a vague replacement sound deeper than a concrete first point.

Keep a contrast only when both sides contain distinct, necessary information.

### Fake collaboration

“Let us explore” and “let us unpack” imply a joint activity when the writer is simply presenting information.

Use a direct heading or statement unless the reader is genuinely being guided through an exercise.

### Corporate fog

Terms such as ecosystem, synergy, alignment, holistic, scalable, future-ready, and value creation can conceal the actual work.

Name the process, tool, person, or result instead.

## 4. Structural habits

### Automatic groups of three

Three items appear because the rhythm feels complete, not because the subject has three parts.

Check whether every item is distinct and necessary. Use two, four, or an unnumbered explanation when that is more accurate.

### Dramatic fragments

Clusters of short sentences create artificial suspense: “The result? A complete shift. Everything changed.”

Use fragments deliberately and sparingly. Recombine them when ordinary prose is clearer.

### Uniform rhythm

Sentences and paragraphs repeat the same length and shape. The prose feels assembled even when every sentence is grammatical.

Vary rhythm according to meaning, not at random.

### Redundant summaries

Each section ends by repeating its opening, and the conclusion restates the introduction without adding a decision or consequence.

Delete summaries that do not advance the argument.

### Rigid outline

Everything becomes “first, second, third, in conclusion” even when the material has a more natural order.

Use chronology, cause and effect, priority, contrast, or a direct answer when those structures fit better.

### Forced redemption

Every failure becomes a lesson, opportunity, or source of growth. Sometimes the accurate conclusion is that something failed and should not be repeated.

### Bullets as camouflage

Prose is split into many bold-labelled bullets that repeat broad benefits without evidence.

Collapse the list into prose. If little remains, the problem is substance rather than format.

### Repeated trailing clauses

Sentences end with loose “-ing” phrases that restate the claim or add grand significance.

Delete the clause and keep it deleted if no meaning disappears.

## 5. Tone problems

### Constant enthusiasm

Every update is exciting, remarkable, or inspiring, including routine changes.

Match emotional intensity to the event.

### Default optimism

Risks are softened, setbacks are reframed, and every outlook is positive without evidence.

Report negative or mixed results plainly.

### Excessive formality

Routine communication sounds like a ceremonial letter. Replace stock politeness with the level of courtesy the relationship requires.

### Performed candour

Phrases such as “I will be honest” or staged admissions create the appearance of vulnerability without adding a real observation.

Keep genuine first-person judgement. Remove signals that merely announce authenticity.

### Voice without a person

The writing has a polished tone but no clear viewpoint, decision, limitation, or experience behind it.

Restore the author's real position when one has been supplied. Never manufacture one.

## 6. Formatting and visual defaults

These are not inherently bad. Treat them as slop when they cluster and substitute for a specific design idea or useful content.

### Text formatting

- repeated em dashes used as automatic drama;
- decorative bolding inside ordinary paragraphs;
- emoji in professional copy without a clear audience reason;
- all-caps labels and excessive title case;
- long bullet stacks that flatten nuance.

### Deck and interface defaults

- a generic “build the future” or “unlock the power” hero;
- purple or blue gradients chosen without a brand reason;
- glass-like panels, pill badges, and rounded cards applied everywhere;
- three identical icon cards with broad benefit labels;
- unsupported `10x`, uptime, or customer-count banners;
- one default geometric sans-serif used for every level of text;
- stock imagery that decorates but does not explain;
- a deck that only “informs” and never asks for a decision;
- a layout reusable for any product after changing the logo.

Repair visual slop by starting from the content, brand, audience, and decision. Use a motif because it communicates something, not because it is a familiar default.

## 7. Red herrings

Do not condemn text because it contains one of these:

- an em dash;
- a formal or unfashionable word;
- correct grammar and no typos;
- contractions or no contractions;
- a three-part list;
- bullets;
- a polished sentence;
- an unusual metaphor;
- a word currently associated with a particular model.

Good writers use all of them. Look for repeated habits plus weak substance.

## 8. Repair tests

### Deletion test

Remove the transition, modifier, final clause, summary, or formatting. If meaning does not change, leave it out.

### Substitution test

Swap a buzzword or benefit for another. If the sentence still seems equally plausible, it is probably too generic.

### Address test

Ask where the claim comes from: a source, result, owner, date, decision, or direct observation. If it has no address, flag it.

### Read-aloud test

Read the sentence at normal speed. Rewrite phrases that sound ceremonial, breathless, or unlike the intended speaker.

### Cold-reader test

Ask what a reader can do, decide, understand, or verify after reading the passage. If the answer is “nothing,” revise the substance before polishing the style.
